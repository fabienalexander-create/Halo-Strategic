## SECTION ONE: Halo Philosophy

### Halo's First Law

> Diagnosis before prescription. Always.

Every chapter below orbits this sentence, and everything built after this document should stay consistent with it: does this stage, metric, product, or recommendation assume diagnosis happens first, or does it quietly skip to prescription?

### The Spine

Seven chapters, one argument, each reinforcing the same idea from a different angle:

- **Why Halo exists** → Businesses make poor decisions because they diagnose badly.
- **What's broken in the industry** → The industry is incentivised to sell solutions before diagnosis.
- **Why businesses fail to grow** → Commercial friction compounds quietly until growth stalls.
- **The biggest misconception** → Businesses think growth comes from adding more, instead of recovering what they're already losing.
- **What makes Halo different** → Halo is loyal to the diagnosis, not the service.
- **What Halo refuses to do** → Principles are only real if you're willing to lose revenue to keep them.
- **The north star** → Growth is the product of a business working in alignment.

### Chapter 1: Why Halo Exists

Halo exists because I became tired of watching good businesses make bad decisions with incomplete information.

Over the years I've seen businesses spend thousands on websites, advertising, AI tools, CRMs and marketing agencies, only to end up exactly where they started. Not because those things don't work, but because they were solving the wrong problem.

The industry has become obsessed with solutions before diagnosis. If sales are down, run ads. If leads are slow, buy software. If revenue stalls, redesign the website. If customers leave, automate follow-up. Everyone is selling an answer before they've properly understood the question.

I don't believe businesses fail because they lack marketing. I believe they fail because commercial problems compound quietly. A slow lead response isn't just a sales issue. Poor retention isn't just a customer service issue. Weak reporting isn't just a dashboard issue. They're symptoms of a business whose commercial systems are no longer working together.

Most agencies only see the part they're paid to fix. Halo was built to see the whole system. Our job isn't to sell marketing. Our job is to help leadership teams understand where commercial friction really exists, what it's costing them, and what should actually be fixed first. Sometimes that leads to marketing. Sometimes it leads to sales. Sometimes it leads to operations. Sometimes the answer is to change nothing at all.

We don't believe every business needs another supplier. We believe every business deserves a clear diagnosis before making another investment. If Halo succeeds, it won't be because we've convinced businesses to buy more services. It will be because we've helped them make better decisions.

That is why Halo exists.

### Chapter 2: What's Broken About the Marketing Industry

The marketing industry doesn't have a talent problem. It has an incentive problem. Most agencies are paid to sell a service, not to solve a problem. That single fact quietly distorts almost everything that follows.

When an agency's revenue depends on selling SEO, every diagnosis tends to conclude the business needs more SEO. When an agency's revenue depends on selling paid ads, every diagnosis tends to conclude the business needs more paid ads. That's not necessarily dishonesty. It's structural bias.

Imagine a doctor who only ever prescribes the one medication their practice happens to sell, regardless of the diagnosis. We'd immediately recognise something was wrong. Yet in marketing, that pattern is treated as completely normal.

The result is an industry that measures success by outputs, not outcomes. Clicks, traffic, followers, impressions, engagement. Those numbers can be useful signals. They are not, on their own, evidence that a business is actually growing. We've seen businesses generate more leads than they can practically respond to. We've seen websites get more traffic while conversion rates fall. We've seen social followings grow while revenue stays completely flat. None of that is growth. It's activity that looks like growth.

At Halo, we believe the industry has it backwards. Diagnosis should come first, execution second. And sometimes, the most valuable thing we can tell a client is that they don't need more marketing at all. If the conclusion is "don't spend your money here," that should be considered a successful engagement, not a failed sale.

### Chapter 3: Why Businesses Really Fail to Grow

Businesses rarely fail because of a single catastrophic mistake. They fail because small commercial problems are allowed to compound. Growth doesn't usually stop overnight. It slows quietly. A lead waits another day for a callback. A proposal sits in someone's inbox. A customer leaves without anyone asking why. A sales meeting ends without clear next steps. Reporting arrives two weeks late. Marketing generates enquiries that operations can't fulfil. None of these moments feels significant on its own. That's what makes them dangerous.

Many leaders believe growth is about finding the next opportunity. We believe it's usually about removing the next constraint. The first question shouldn't be "what should we add?" It should be "what's preventing the business we already have from performing as it should?"

Growth isn't created by activity. It's created by alignment. When leadership, sales, marketing, operations and customer experience all reinforce one another, momentum builds naturally. When they work against one another, every department works harder for diminishing returns.

We don't assume the biggest problem is the one making the most noise. We assume the biggest problem is the one creating the most downstream friction. Businesses don't grow because they do everything. They grow because they identify what matters most, fix it properly, and allow the rest of the business to move again.

### Chapter 4: The Biggest Misconception About Growth

The biggest misconception business owners have is that growth comes from adding something new: a new agency, a new CRM, a new salesperson, a new AI tool, a new website, a new strategy. The assumption is always the same: if we add the right thing, growth will follow. Sometimes that's true. Most of the time, it isn't.

Growth doesn't usually stall because a business lacks another tool. It stalls because the business has stopped converting the potential it already has. Businesses leak value every day: leads go unanswered, customers don't come back, processes become inconsistent, data is collected but never used to make decisions. None of those problems are solved by adding another layer of complexity. In fact, complexity often hides the real issue.

Very few people are rewarded for telling a business "you don't need more, you need better alignment." That can be an uncomfortable message. It isn't as exciting as a new growth strategy. But it's often the truth.

We believe businesses grow in two stages. First, they recover the value they're already creating but failing to capture. Only then do they focus on creating more. There's little point pouring more water into a bucket that's already leaking. Growth isn't something you bolt onto a business. It's what happens when the business is no longer fighting itself.

### Chapter 5: What Makes Halo Different

Halo isn't built around a marketing service. It's built around a way of thinking. Most agencies begin with what they sell: advertising, SEO, websites, automation, content. We begin with the commercial system. Before asking how to generate more demand, we ask whether the business is capable of converting, retaining and learning from the demand it already has. If the biggest constraint isn't marketing, recommending marketing isn't good advice, it might be good business for the agency, but it isn't good business for the client.

We don't measure our success by how many services we deliver. We measure it by whether we helped a business make a better commercial decision. We're comfortable with that because our loyalty isn't to a service. It's to the diagnosis.

Many consultancies focus on acceleration. We focus on removing resistance. Acceleration applied to a business full of friction simply helps it reach the wrong destination faster. That often looks slower in the beginning. In reality, it's more durable.

We also believe expertise should create clarity, not dependency. A good consultant shouldn't become indispensable, they should leave the business understanding itself better than it did before. The best outcome isn't that a client needs Halo forever. The best outcome is that, after working together, they need us less than they did when we first met.

Most firms define themselves by what they do. Halo is defining itself by how it thinks. A competitor can copy a service, a pricing model, or even a website. It's far more difficult to copy a decision-making philosophy that's consistently reflected in every recommendation, every framework, and every client engagement. That's the foundation that makes Halo distinctive over the long term.

### Chapter 6: What Halo Refuses to Do

Principles only become real when they cost you something. Every business has values. Very few have non-negotiables. A non-negotiable is a principle you're willing to lose revenue to protect. These are ours:

**We will not prescribe before we diagnose.** A rushed recommendation isn't expertise. It's guesswork.

**We will not optimise the wrong thing.** Speed is only valuable when you're moving in the right direction.

**We will not sell certainty where certainty doesn't exist.** We won't manufacture confidence simply because confidence is easier to sell than honesty.

**We will not hide behind vanity metrics.** If reporting doesn't help leadership make a better commercial decision, it isn't useful reporting.

**We will not create dependency.** If our work only functions while we're in the room, we've built dependency instead of capability.

**We will not recommend work that isn't needed.** Sometimes the right answer is "don't spend your money here." Trust compounds. So does regret. We're interested in the first one.

**We will not confuse activity with progress.** Activity is visible. Progress is measurable. We care about the second.

**We will tell clients what they need to hear, not what they want to hear.** Our responsibility is to the truth as we understand it, supported by evidence.

If following these costs us a project, that's the price of keeping them. Principles that only apply when they're profitable aren't principles at all.

### Chapter 7: The Sentence

Businesses don't have a marketing problem. They have a **commercial alignment problem.** Marketing is only one part of a business, sales is another, operations, customer experience, leadership, finance. When those parts work together, growth feels almost inevitable. When they work against one another, no amount of marketing can compensate.

We don't start with marketing. We start by asking: "where is the business losing momentum?" Marketing didn't create most commercial problems. Marketing can't solve them on its own. Businesses grow because the entire commercial system begins pulling in the same direction, that's commercial alignment.

> Businesses don't have a marketing problem. They have a commercial alignment problem. Marketing creates attention. Alignment creates growth.

### The Diagnostic Language

Four related terms, not synonyms competing for one slot, a causal chain, one journey rather than four concepts:

- **Commercial Leakage** — where value escapes the business. Observable symptoms. Lost opportunities.
- **Commercial Friction** — the resistance causing the leakage. Processes that slow momentum. Misalignment between functions.
- **Commercial Constraints** — the root cause limiting growth. The bottleneck that matters most. The thing to remove first.
- **Commercial Alignment** — the outcome. A business whose systems reinforce each other instead of working against each other.

**Leakage → Friction → Constraint → Alignment.** You observe leakage, trace it to friction, find the constraint causing that friction, remove it, and the result is alignment. Alignment isn't a fifth item in the list; it's what the other three, done in order, produce. Most businesses invent frameworks first and bend the philosophy to fit them; Halo built the vocabulary before the frameworks existed, which is why it now threads consistently through the Master Framework, the Friction Map, and Halo Indicators below rather than each inventing its own language.

## SECTION TWO: Core Principles

Extracted from `/how-halo-thinks`, already live, not invented fresh:

1. **Growth is usually constrained by systems before it's constrained by marketing.** Most businesses don't need more leads. They need fewer bottlenecks.
2. **Reporting only earns its place if it changes a decision.** If a report doesn't change what happens next, it isn't doing its job. It's admin.
3. **Build the protection before you need it.** The best systems prevent problems. They don't just measure them after the fact.
4. **Reduce dependency by increasing capability.** The goal isn't to be indispensable. It's to leave a business more capable than you found it.
5. **Complexity is only useful once it's simple enough to act on.** If a founder or a board can't act on it, the analysis isn't finished yet.
6. **Capability should be built into people, not just into systems.** Dashboards don't grow a team. The people running them do.
7. **The method should transfer across industries.** The industry isn't the expertise. The approach is.
8. **Judgement sharpens by sitting in every seat at the table.** Publisher, agency, in-house, founder, each seat teaches something the others can't.
9. **Responsibility should expand deliberately, not by accident.** Growth in scope should be earned and chosen, not something that just happens to you.

## SECTION THREE: The Halo Commercial Growth System (Master Framework)

Answers: **how does Halo actually diagnose and improve a business?** Seven stages, specified by Fabien directly, each checked against the Diagnostic Language hierarchy and grounded against a real Selected Engagement.

1. **Observe** — Identify commercial leakage: where value is escaping. *Evidenced by: We Told the Client the Market Was Too Small; Fixing Sales Follow-up Before Buying More Leads.*
2. **Diagnose** — Understand the friction creating that leakage. *Evidenced by: Fixing Sales Follow-up Before Buying More Leads; From Reacting to Leading.*
3. **Prioritise** — Identify the primary constraint. *Evidenced by: We Moved the Client Off an Unstable Platform; Good Reporting Changes Decisions, Not Just Dashboards.*
4. **Align** — Bring leadership, sales, marketing and operations around the constraint. *Evidenced by: From Reacting to Leading.*
5. **Execute** — Implement the highest-leverage changes. *Evidenced by: Fixing Sales Follow-up Before Buying More Leads; We Moved the Client Off an Unstable Platform.*
6. **Measure** — Confirm whether friction has reduced and alignment has improved. *Evidenced by: Good Reporting Changes Decisions, Not Just Dashboards; The Data That Was Never Missing.*
7. **Repeat** — Growth is continuous because new constraints emerge.

Stages 1–3 are the diagnostic sequence (Leakage → Friction → Constraint), stages 4–6 turn diagnosis into a result (Constraint → Alignment, confirmed), stage 7 acknowledges alignment is maintained, not finished. Satisfies Halo's First Law by construction: diagnosis (1–3) is structurally first.

*Open: Stages 3 and 4 have thinner direct evidence than the others. No per-stage failure indicators exist yet, that would mean inventing failure patterns rather than extracting them from real cases, with only six cases to draw from so far.*

## SECTION FOUR: Commercial Leakage Framework

**What is Commercial Leakage?** "Most revenue doesn't disappear in one dramatic failure. It leaks through a dozen small, unowned handoffs between departments, a slow response to a new lead, a CRM nobody trusts enough to use properly, a reporting gap that means two departments are arguing over two different numbers." (already published on `/commercial-audit`)

**The six constraint areas:**

| Constraint Area | Diagnostic question |
|---|---|
| Commercial Strategy | Is there one growth plan leadership agrees on, and do the unit economics actually support it? |
| Acquisition Systems | Which channels produce customers, versus which produce leads that never convert? |
| Conversion & Sales Systems | How long between a lead arriving and a human actually engaging it? |
| Operations & CRM Systems | Does the CRM reflect what's actually happening, or is it a system nobody trusts? |
| Retention & Client Experience | What's the real churn rate, against what the business believes it is? |
| Decision & Reporting Systems | Does leadership have one trusted number per key metric, or six different versions of the same one? |

**Real cases mapped to leak type:** We Told the Client the Market Was Too Small (Commercial Strategy) · Fixing Sales Follow-up Before Buying More Leads (Conversion & Sales) · Good Reporting Changes Decisions (Decision & Reporting) · We Moved the Client Off an Unstable Platform (Acquisition) · From Reacting to Leading (Operations & CRM) · The Data That Was Never Missing (Operations & CRM / Decision & Reporting).

**The Halo Score:** already a real deliverable on `/commercial-audit`, "every commercial pillar scored on evidence quality, performance gap, and commercial leverage."

*Open: a separate "Commercial Leakage %" metric, distinct from the Halo Score, has no formula, that's a methodology-design decision requiring Fabien's judgement, not invented here.*

## SECTION FIVE: Revenue Friction Map

The practical, diagnostic-tool version of the friction layer: not "where is value lost" (Section Four), but "where is the resistance causing it." Seven domains, cross-checked against Section Four's six areas so both sections read the same business through two lenses:

- **Leadership** — does leadership agree on one growth plan, or is every function quietly working to its own version of "growth"? *(We Told the Client the Market Was Too Small)*
- **Marketing** — are channels producing customers, or leads that never convert? *(We Moved the Client Off an Unstable Platform)*
- **Sales** — what happens to a lead in the first hour it arrives? *(Fixing Sales Follow-up Before Buying More Leads)*
- **Customer Experience** — when a customer leaves, does anyone find out why? *(Thin evidence, no dedicated Selected Engagement yet.)*
- **Operations** — does the CRM reflect what's actually happening, or is it a system nobody trusts? *(From Reacting to Leading)*
- **Data & Reporting** — if two people pulled the same metric today, would they get the same number? *(Good Reporting Changes Decisions; The Data That Was Never Missing)*
- **Finance** — not yet evidenced. No corresponding Section Four area, no Selected Engagement framed around pricing, margin, or cash flow.

## SECTION SIX: Growth Maturity Model

### The Measurement Rule

> Do not define maturity by business size, revenue, employee count, or years in operation. Define maturity by the quality of commercial decision-making.

A £300k business can be highly mature if it makes disciplined, evidence-based decisions. A £20 million business can still be reactive if it's constantly chasing symptoms instead of understanding constraints.

### The Five Levels

**Level 1 — Reactive.** The business runs the founder. Everything is urgent. No systems. No visibility. Constant firefighting.

**Level 2 — Aware.** The founder starts recognising recurring problems. They know something isn't working. They just don't know why. Still solving symptoms.

**Level 3 — Diagnosing.** Instead of "we need more leads," the business asks "what evidence tells us that's the biggest constraint?" Decisions become evidence-based.

**Level 4 — Optimising.** The business already understands itself. Now it's improving deliberately: testing, measuring, refining, removing friction continuously.

**Level 5 — Aligned.** Marketing, sales, operations, leadership, technology, customer experience, all working toward the same commercial outcomes. The business doesn't just grow. It compounds.

**Level 5, Aligned, is the same concept as Commercial Alignment in Section One.** This model describes a business's standing capability to run the Leakage → Friction → Constraint → Alignment sequence on its own, over time, not a coincidentally similar word.

### The Signal Between Levels

- **Reactive → Aware:** the founder stops blaming external factors and starts recognising internal patterns.
- **Aware → Diagnosing:** the business asks "why is this happening?" instead of "how do we fix it?"
- **Diagnosing → Optimising:** recommendations are based on evidence rather than instinct; metrics begin driving priorities.
- **Optimising → Aligned:** departments stop optimising for themselves and start optimising for the business.

*Illustrated by: From Reacting to Leading (the Level 1 → 3/4 transition, in its own title). Open: no evidenced answer yet on what regression looks like (can a business slip back from Aligned?); not asked, not assumed.*

## SECTION SEVEN: Halo Indicators

Formerly "Halo Metrics," renamed because "metrics" implies pure numbers, and the Philosophy explicitly argues against that instinct (Chapters 2, 6). A founder finally agreeing on one priority is real progress even before it's a KPI.

### Halo Indicators vs. Engagement Metrics

**Halo Indicators** are universal, they'd make sense on every Diagnostic regardless of industry, because they measure commercial capability, the same thing the Growth Maturity Model measures.

**Engagement Metrics** are specific to the client's business and emerge from the Diagnostic itself, not standardised. Time from lead arrival to first human contact, quote turnaround, utilisation, repeat purchase rate, proposal conversion, and the four homepage Evidence stats (14→1, 43%, 3→6-8, ~25%) all live here, not among the universal Indicators.

### The Four Halo Indicators

1. **Visibility** — do we actually know what's happening? Not the numbers themselves, whether visibility exists at all. *Precondition for identifying Leakage at all.*
2. **Decision Confidence** — "if you made your three biggest commercial decisions again today, would you make the same ones?" *The observable version of the Aware → Diagnosing transition.*
3. **Constraint Resolution** — did we identify the right bottleneck? Was it removed? Did another become visible? *The Master Framework's Prioritise → Execute → Measure → Repeat loop, as a capability check.*
4. **Alignment** — are leadership, sales, marketing, and operations actually pulling in the same direction? *The same Alignment as Section One and Level 5 of the Growth Maturity Model.*

Three independently-built sections (One, Six, Seven) converge on the same end-state, described three ways: a diagnostic sequence's resolution, a business's standing capability, and a thing to check for on every engagement. Not a coincidence, the grammar of Halo holding together.

*Open: no numeric scoring assigned to the four Indicators yet, a later decision, not invented here.*

## SECTION EIGHT: Glossary

- **Commercial Growth Consultancy** — how Halo describes itself, used consistently site-wide, not "marketing agency."
- **Commercial Health Check** — the free, 10-minute self-assessment, Stage 1 of the Product Journey.
- **Commercial Diagnostic** — the paid, structured diagnostic session ($995), Stage 1 of the Product Journey. *(Updated 2026-07-26, Sprint 5.6: previously free; repriced per Fabien's direct approval as Product Owner.)*
- **Commercial Audit** — Halo's flagship engagement ($7,500+, scope-dependent), Stage 2 of the Product Journey. *(Updated 2026-07-26, Sprint 5.6: previously a fixed £3,000.)*
- **Commercial Leak** — a specific, named point where revenue is lost through an unowned handoff, not a vague "inefficiency."
- **The Halo Score** — the scored output of a Commercial Audit, evidence quality × performance gap × commercial leverage per pillar.
- **Constraint** — the single thing currently limiting growth, deliberately distinct from "problem"; Halo's method assumes one constraint dominates, not an average of many issues.
- **Selected Engagements** — Halo's term for its case studies, deliberately not "testimonials" or "success stories," since several document genuinely unconfirmed outcomes.
- **Halo's First Law** — "Diagnosis before prescription. Always." The single sentence every framework, product, and recommendation should stay consistent with.
- **Commercial Leakage / Friction / Constraint / Alignment** — the Diagnostic Language hierarchy (Section One): leakage is where value escapes, friction is what slows momentum, constraint is what limits growth, alignment is the achieved end-state.
- **Halo Indicators** — the four universal measures of commercial capability (Visibility, Decision Confidence, Constraint Resolution, Alignment), distinct from Engagement Metrics, which are business-specific and not standardised.
- **The Product Journey** — the six-stage client path (Discover, Investigate, Design, Implement, Optimise, Partnership), see Section Ten.
