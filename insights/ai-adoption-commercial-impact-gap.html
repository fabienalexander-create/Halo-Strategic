AI Adoption Doubled. Commercial Impact Mostly Didn't

AI adoption among UK businesses doubled this year. Thirty-two per cent of firms were using at least one AI technology in 2026, up from sixteen per cent in 2025, according to the government's own survey of the market. Ask the same businesses what that adoption actually bought them, and the picture gets a lot less tidy. Half of the ones using AI say it helped productivity, but call the gain modest. Only fourteen per cent think AI tools matter enough to their operations to call them high or critical importance. Nearly two-thirds saw no measurable change to their costs at all.

None of that is an argument against AI. It is an argument against treating adoption and commercial impact as the same thing, which is exactly the mistake Halo sees founders make with every other tool category too. The businesses getting a return are not the ones that bought the most AI. They are the ones that worked out which commercial constraint the tool was actually meant to move, before they bought anything.

What the 2026 numbers actually show

The Department for Science, Innovation and Technology published its AI Adoption Survey Report 2026 on 29 October 2026, based on interviews with 3,503 UK businesses plus 50 in-depth follow-ups. The headline is the doubling already mentioned. The detail is more useful. Adoption is pulled up hard by scale: fifty-two per cent of larger firms use AI, against the thirty-two per cent average across the whole business population, and spend on AI rises with company size too. The sectors leading uptake are information and communication (sixty-one per cent), real estate (forty-five per cent), and business services and administration (thirty-nine per cent), which tells you adoption is still concentrated where digital infrastructure and data discipline were already in reasonable shape, not spread evenly across the economy.

On impact, the report's own conclusion is blunt: AI is "functioning primarily as a support tool, augmenting existing activities rather than redefining business models." Seventy-one per cent of adopters cited efficiency and productivity as the main driver for taking AI on, and fifty per cent of adopters reported an overall productivity increase, though the report describes those gains as generally modest. On cost, the picture is flatter still: twenty-four per cent saw operating costs fall, nine per cent saw them rise, and sixty-four per cent saw no immediate financial effect either way. The barrier most commonly cited, particularly among the sixty-five per cent of non-adopters who have not taken AI on at all, is a lack of a clear business case or proven return on investment. That is not a technology complaint. It is a commercial one.

The pattern underneath: why pilots don't pay off

The UK data lines up with a more pointed finding from MIT's NANDA research initiative, reported by Fortune in August 2025. Drawing on 150 leadership interviews, a survey of 350 employees and an analysis of 300 public AI deployments, the researchers found that around ninety-five per cent of generative AI pilots failed to show any measurable effect on profit and loss, while roughly five per cent achieved rapid revenue gains. The gap between those two groups had very little to do with which model or vendor a business chose. The report's own framing is that the failure is "the clearest manifestation of the GenAI Divide," and the researchers point to what they call a learning gap: generic tools that do not adapt to a business's actual workflow stall out in enterprise use, however capable the underlying model is.

Two details matter most if you are the one signing off an AI budget. More than half of enterprise generative AI spend goes to sales and marketing tools, yet the researchers found the strongest return in back-office automation instead: cutting agency costs, removing outsourced admin, tightening processes that already had clear rules. And buying from a specialist vendor or partner succeeded roughly two-thirds of the time, against barely a third for internal builds. Put together, the pattern is not "AI doesn't work." It is "most businesses point AI at the least settled part of the business, and build what they should have bought."

The same constraint Halo always finds

This is the same failure mode behind AI doesn't solve the reporting problem, it multiplies it: a tool makes something faster to produce, and the business mistakes that speed for progress, without ever deciding what decision the output was supposed to change. AI adoption numbers measure exactly that kind of speed. Productivity, cost, and business-case clarity measure whether a decision actually changed, and on the UK's own figures, those three lag a long way behind adoption.

Founders tend to reach for a new tool when growth stalls, because a tool is a decision you can make this week, and a commercial diagnosis is a decision you have been avoiding for six months. Leakage, friction, and constraint aren't the same thing, and neither is the fix. If the real constraint is an undiagnosed sales process, a messy handoff between marketing and sales, or data nobody trusts enough to act on, see check the plumbing before you touch the strategy, then AI applied on top of it does not remove the constraint. It automates the mess at a higher volume, which is precisely what the MIT researchers found happening inside businesses that pointed generative AI at ambiguous, judgement-heavy front-office work instead of a well-defined back-office process.

What this means for how you buy AI, not whether you do

None of this is a case for waiting AI out. The fourteen per cent of UK businesses that already call it high or critical to their operations are not wrong to be there, and the gap between the ninety-five per cent and the five per cent in the MIT research is one any business can choose which side of. Three things separate them. Pick the process with the clearest rules, not the most visible upside: back-office work outperforms sales and marketing because the rules are already settled. Buy a tool built for the workflow rather than building your own, since bought and partnered solutions succeed at roughly double the rate of internal builds. And decide in advance what measurable outcome the tool is meant to move, lead response time, quote turnaround, cost per qualified enquiry, so that six months on you are not left reporting that it feels faster with no way to say whether it earned its keep.

That last point is the one founders skip most often, and the one a diagnosis exists to catch before the spend happens rather than after.

FAQ

What people ask about AI and commercial growth.

Is AI actually improving results for UK businesses?

Partly. The government's AI Adoption Survey Report 2026 found half of adopters reported a productivity increase, but described the gains as generally modest, and only fourteen per cent of UK businesses consider AI tools of high or critical importance to their operations. Adoption has grown fast; measurable commercial impact has grown much more slowly.

Why do most generative AI pilots fail to deliver a return?

MIT's NANDA research initiative, reported by Fortune in 2025, found around ninety-five per cent of generative AI pilots showed no measurable effect on profit and loss. The researchers pointed to a learning gap: generic tools that do not adapt to a business's actual workflow, rather than any shortfall in the underlying models.

Should a smaller business build its own AI tools or buy them?

The evidence favours buying. The same MIT research found that buying from a specialist vendor or partnering with one succeeded roughly two-thirds of the time, while internal builds succeeded barely a third as often, largely because specialist tools are built around a known workflow rather than general-purpose capability.

How do I know if an AI tool is actually working commercially?

Decide what it is meant to change before you buy it, a specific number like lead response time or cost per qualified enquiry, then check that number afterwards. If the honest answer six months on is "it feels faster" with no measurable shift, the tool has not yet earned its keep, whatever it cost.
