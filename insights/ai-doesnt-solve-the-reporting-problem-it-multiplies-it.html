AI Doesn't Solve the Reporting Problem. It Multiplies It

Every dashboard tool on the market now has an AI feature bolted onto it somewhere: auto-generated summaries, AI-written commentary, a chatbot that will draft you a report in seconds instead of the twenty minutes it used to take a person. The pitch is always the same. Reporting was slow. Now it's fast. Problem solved.

It isn't, because speed was never the actual constraint. Halo's second core principle set the real bar for reporting well before AI made producing it easier: reporting only earns its place if it changes a decision. Not if it's accurate. Not if it arrives instantly. If it vanished tomorrow, would a different decision get made because of it existing? AI changes how fast a business can generate something that looks like an answer to that question. It does nothing to answer the question itself.

If anything, it makes the underlying problem worse, not better, because it removes the one thing that used to act as a natural brake on producing reporting nobody uses: effort. When a report took someone half a day to pull together, there was at least a mild commercial cost to building one nobody read. That cost is disappearing. A dashboard, a summary, a commentary paragraph can now be generated in the time it takes to type a prompt, which means the volume of reporting a business produces can grow with almost nothing stopping it, while the number of decisions that reporting actually changes stays exactly where it was.

Two Halo engagements already showed what failing this test looks like, neither one involving AI at all. An international education provider's account was escalated internally as underperforming while it was actually sitting against its defined guardrail the whole time, the reporting was accurate, just too dense to act on. A high-spend rehabilitation facility had complete, accurate verification data arriving every month that nobody had converted into anything decision-usable. Both cases prove the same thing an AI-generated report would still have to clear: correct isn't the bar. Useful is. A faster route to correct-but-unused reporting is not progress, it's the same failure delivered at a higher volume.

Halo uses AI in its own operation, including to produce first drafts of material like this one. That's not a caveat, it's the actual test in practice. A draft an AI tool produces doesn't get published, and no client-facing recommendation gets made from AI-generated analysis, until a person has checked it against real evidence and decided it's actually worth acting on. That review step is the whole principle, applied to Halo's own output before it ever gets applied to a client's. If a business claims to be "using AI for reporting" without a version of that same check in place, what's actually happened is that the volume went up and the decision-changing rate stayed flat, or got worse, because more material now needs reviewing by the same number of people who were already too stretched to read what they had.

This isn't an argument against using AI in reporting. It's an argument against mistaking the tool for the fix. The same test Halo already runs against a human-built dashboard applies without modification to an AI-built one: does looking at this change what anyone does next. AI can help build the answer faster once that question's been asked properly. It can't ask the question for you, and increasingly, it's very good at producing something that looks finished enough that nobody thinks to ask it at all.

A short check worth running before adding AI to any part of a reporting stack:

  • Name the decision first, not the report. Before asking an AI tool to build or summarise anything, write down the specific decision it's meant to inform. If nobody can name one, the tool is about to automate a habit, not a function.
  • Check who actually reviews the output. If AI-generated commentary goes straight from the tool to a client, a board, or a leadership meeting with nobody checking it against what's actually happening, that's the same unread-dashboard problem, just harder to notice because the format looks more polished.
  • Watch the volume, not just the quality. The real risk isn't that AI-generated reporting is wrong. It's that more of it gets produced simply because producing it got cheap, and nobody notices the decision-changing rate didn't move with it.
  • Keep asking the same question the principle always asked. If this report vanished tomorrow, would a different decision get made because of it? AI doesn't change the answer. It just changes how quickly you can produce something that avoids asking it.

AI made reporting faster to produce. It didn't make it any more likely to change a decision, and for a while, that's going to make the problem harder to see, not easier to fix.

This sits directly underneath the same test run in Does Your Reporting Actually Change Any Decisions? and anchors to Visibility, the first of Halo's four Indicators, which was never about whether data exists, only about whether it exists in a form someone can act on. AI changes how the data gets produced. It doesn't touch whether it's visible in that sense. The same test is one of six covered in Six Places Revenue Quietly Leaks, under Decision & Reporting Systems, and the pattern it's closest to is the one described in The Reporting Problem Hiding Behind Good Performance, a business can look like it's solving a reporting problem while the actual gap, between what's produced and what's acted on, stays exactly the same size.

FAQ

What founders ask about AI and reporting.

Is Halo saying businesses shouldn't use AI for reporting?

No. The argument isn't against the tool, it's against treating faster production as the same thing as a better decision. AI can genuinely help once a business knows which decision a report is meant to inform. It can't supply that clarity on its own.

Does Halo use AI internally?

Yes, including to produce first drafts of material like this one. Nothing generated that way goes out, and no recommendation gets made from it, until a person has checked it against real evidence and decided it actually earns its place, the same test described here, applied to Halo's own output first.

How is an AI-generated report different from a human-built one that also fails the test?

Mechanically, it isn't, both can be accurate and still change nothing. What's different is the economics. A human-built report that nobody uses at least cost someone real time to produce, which puts a natural ceiling on how much of it a business generates. AI removes that ceiling, so the volume of unused reporting can grow much faster than anyone reviewing it can keep up with.

What should we actually check before rolling out an AI reporting tool?

Start with the decision, not the dashboard. Name the specific decision the report is meant to inform, confirm someone is actually reviewing the output against real evidence rather than passing it straight through, and watch the volume of reporting being produced, not just its accuracy. If the amount of reporting is going up and the decisions being made because of it aren't, the tool is amplifying the existing problem, not fixing it.
